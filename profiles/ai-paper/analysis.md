# Evaluation goal

Evaluate how much a preprint deserves the attention of a practitioner who follows AI research closely but does not have time to read every paper. Assume the reader builds with these models rather than only studying them.

# Scoring rubric

- **9-10: Must read.** A result that changes what practitioners should do — a genuinely new capability, a method that beats strong baselines by a wide and well-evidenced margin, a benchmark or negative result that invalidates a common assumption, or a landmark release from a major lab.
- **7-8: High value.** A solid, well-evidenced contribution: a method with convincing ablations, a useful new benchmark or dataset, a careful empirical study that settles a real question, or a survey that meaningfully consolidates a fast-moving area.
- **5-6: Interesting.** A reasonable contribution with narrow scope, modest gains, limited evaluation, or a familiar idea applied to a new domain.
- **3-4: Low priority.** Incremental variation on a well-explored theme, thin evaluation, or claims not supported by the reported evidence.
- **0-2: Noise.** Trivial, off-topic, or with no discernible contribution.

# Evaluation guidance

Weigh novelty of the idea, strength and honesty of the evaluation, reproducibility (released code, weights, or data), and how much the result would change a practitioner's decisions. Judge the evidence rather than the framing: discount sweeping claims backed by one small benchmark, and do not reward a paper for asserting state of the art without comparison to current strong baselines.

Author affiliation and volume are weak signals — do not treat a well-known lab or a long author list as evidence of importance on their own. Topic popularity is not importance; a crowded area needs a sharper result to earn a high score.

Only the title and abstract are usually available. Score what the abstract actually supports, and keep uncertainty rather than inventing detail.

Use three to five specific topic tags.
